# Catch-up anatomy: what the evidence says

**Question.** What is the most effective way to restore a Reader's memory and context of the Read Portion, so they can continue at the chapter after the Resume Point? Specifically, what anatomy (section order, structure, length, prose vs lists vs tables vs layers) should a Catch-up have?

**Scope.** This note compares the current anatomy in `prompt.md` (variant A) and the four alternatives in `docs/prototypes/catch-up-anatomy-prototype.html`: B resume-first (inverted pyramid), C "Previously on" narrative prose, D timeline and tables, and E layered 30 s / 3 min / full.

**Method and honesty note.** I checked bibliographic metadata (title, journal, year, DOI) for every DOI-cited paper against Crossref. For most findings I relied on the published abstract, or on how the literature summarizes a classic result. I did not re-read every full text. Claims I could only find in secondary sources are marked **[secondary]**. One point matters most: **no study tests "a document that helps someone resume a novel after a pause of days or weeks."** Every recommendation below is an inference from adjacent research: narrative comprehension, memory, and task interruption. The Confidence notes say how far each inference stretches.

## 1. Findings

### 1.1 What a reader's memory of a story looks like (situation models)

- **Readers remember a model of the situation, not the text.** Comprehension produces three levels of representation: the surface form (exact wording), the textbase (propositions), and the *situation model* (the people, places, events and their relations that the text describes). The situation model is what drives later memory and reasoning. Zwaan, R. A., & Radvansky, G. A. (1998). *Situation models in language comprehension and memory.* Psychological Bulletin, 123(2), 162–185. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.123.2.162 ; Kintsch, W. (1988). *The role of knowledge in discourse comprehension: A construction-integration model.* Psychological Review, 95(2), 163–182. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.95.2.163
- **Surface wording fades quickly; the situation model lasts.** Recognition studies separate the three levels and find that memory for exact wording decays fastest, while memory for the situation persists longest. Kintsch, W., Welsch, D., Schmalhofer, F., & Zimny, S. (1990). *Sentence memory: A theoretical analysis.* Journal of Memory and Language, 29, 133–159. https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596X(90)90069-C ; Fletcher, C. R., & Chrysler, S. T. (1990). *Surface forms, textbases, and situation models: Recognition memory for three types of textual information.* Discourse Processes, 13, 175–190. https://doi.org/10.1080/01638539009544752 **[the decay-rate summary is how later citing work describes these papers; I did not read the full texts]**
- **Readers index a story on five dimensions: time, space, protagonist, causality and intentionality (goals).** The event-indexing model holds that a reader updates the situation model whenever one of these dimensions changes. Events that share more indices are linked more strongly in memory. Zwaan, R. A., Langston, M. C., & Graesser, A. C. (1995). *The construction of situation models in narrative comprehension: An event-indexing model.* Psychological Science, 6(5), 292–297. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1995.tb00513.x ; Zwaan, R. A., Magliano, J. P., & Graesser, A. C. (1995). *Dimensions of situation model construction in narrative comprehension.* JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition, 21(2), 386–397. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.21.2.386
- **Characters' goals keep entities accessible.** Objects and people tied to a protagonist's current goal stay more accessible than other entities. Rinck, M., & Bower, G. H. (1995). *Anaphora resolution and the focus of attention in situation models.* Journal of Memory and Language, 34, 110–131. https://doi.org/10.1006/jmla.1995.1006 . The story's current state is "foregrounded" in working memory. Glenberg, A. M., Meyer, M., & Lindem, K. (1987). *Mental models contribute to foregrounding during text comprehension.* Journal of Memory and Language, 26, 69–83. https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596X(87)90063-5
- **Readers keep both local and global coherence.** While reading the current passage, readers check it against earlier facts, such as a character trait established long before, and notice inconsistencies. Global context therefore matters even for reading the next scene. Albrecht, J. E., & O'Brien, E. J. (1993). *Updating a mental model: Maintaining both local and global coherence.* JEP: LMC, 19(5), 1061–1070. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.19.5.1061

### 1.2 What survives: causal and goal structure

- **The causal chain predicts recall, summary inclusion and importance.** Events on a story's causal chain are recalled more often, included in summaries more often, and rated more important than "dead-end" events. The more causal connections an event has, the stronger this effect. Trabasso, T., & van den Broek, P. (1985). *Causal thinking and the representation of narrative events.* Journal of Memory and Language, 24, 612–630. https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596X(85)90049-X ; Trabasso, T., & Sperry, L. L. (1985). *Causal relatedness and importance of story events.* JML, 24, 595–611. https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596X(85)90048-8 ; van den Broek, P. (1988). *The effects of causal relations and hierarchical position on the importance of story statements.* JML, 27, 1–22. https://doi.org/10.1016/0749-596x(88)90045-9 ; van den Broek, P., & Trabasso, T. (1986). *Causal networks versus goal hierarchies in summarizing text.* Discourse Processes, 9, 1–15. https://doi.org/10.1080/01638538609544628
- **Readers understand stories as goal-directed problem solving.** Statements on the protagonist's goal path are remembered best. Black, J. B., & Bower, G. H. (1980). *Story understanding as problem-solving.* Poetics, 9, 223–250. https://doi.org/10.1016/0304-422X(80)90021-2
- **Readers spontaneously infer causes and goals during reading** ("explanation" and "coherence" assumptions). Graesser, A. C., Singer, M., & Trabasso, T. (1994). *Constructing inferences during narrative text comprehension.* Psychological Review, 101(3), 371–395. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.101.3.371
- **Story grammars: readers expect setting → initiating event → goal → attempt → outcome → reaction.** Recall follows this canonical structure and suffers when it is violated. Mandler, J. M., & Johnson, N. S. (1977). *Remembrance of things parsed: Story structure and recall.* Cognitive Psychology, 9, 111–151. https://doi.org/10.1016/0010-0285(77)90006-8 ; Stein, N. L., & Glenn, C. G. (1979). *An analysis of story comprehension in elementary school children.* In R. O. Freedle (Ed.), *New Directions in Discourse Processing* (pp. 53–120). Ablex. (book chapter, no DOI)
- **Macrostructure: higher-level gist is remembered better than detail (the "levels effect").** Kintsch, W., & van Dijk, T. A. (1978). *Toward a model of text comprehension and production.* Psychological Review, 85(5), 363–394. https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.85.5.363
- **Temporal organization.** When information is organized around time, retrieval from situation models follows that organization. Radvansky, G. A., Zwaan, R. A., Federico, T., & Franklin, N. (1998). *Retrieval from temporally organized situation models.* JEP: LMC, 24(5), 1224–1237. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.24.5.1224

### 1.3 Forgetting a novel over time

- **Memory for a novel declines over time, and recall is better for early material.** In a study where participants read a 10-chapter novel, memory tests by chapter showed better recall for early chapters, plus "bumps" at life-event transitions in the story. Copeland, D. E., Radvansky, G. A., & Goodwin, K. A. (2009). *A novel study: Forgetting curves and the reminiscence bump.* Memory, 17(3), 323–336. https://doi.org/10.1080/09658210902729491 (abstract-level)
- **General forgetting follows a steep early drop that then levels off.** Murre, J. M. J., & Dros, J. (2015). *Replication and analysis of Ebbinghaus' forgetting curve.* PLOS ONE, 10(7), e0120644. https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0120644 . This curve comes from nonsense syllables. Meaningful narrative decays much more slowly at the situation level (§1.1), so I treat it only as background.
- **Serial position: list recall favours the first and last items.** Murdock, B. B. (1962). *The serial position effect of free recall.* Journal of Experimental Psychology, 64(5), 482–488. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0045106 . For a Reader returning after days or weeks, short-term recency is gone. The last chapter is fresh only in the sense that it was encoded most recently and is the context that the next chapter continues.

### 1.4 Narrative vs expository form

- **Stories are understood and recalled better than essays.** A meta-analysis of more than 75 samples and over 33,000 participants found that narrative texts were "more easily understood and better recalled than essays." The result was robust and was not moderated by the study characteristics tested. Mar, R. A., Li, J., Nguyen, A. T. P., & Ta, C. P. (2021). *Memory and comprehension of narrative versus expository texts: A meta-analysis.* Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 28, 732–749. https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-020-01853-1
  - *Caveat:* the studies compare **genres** (story vs essay), not the **same content written as prose vs bullet list**. This is indirect support for writing the plot as connected narrative.
- **"Psychologically privileged."** The phrase is commonly attributed to Graesser's work, for example: Graesser, A. C., Hauft-Smith, K., Cohen, A. D., & Pyles, L. D. (1980). *Advanced outlines, familiarity, and text genre on retention of prose.* Journal of Experimental Education, 48(4), 281–290. https://doi.org/10.1080/00220973.1980.11011745 . I found the exact phrase only in Willingham's popular summary: Willingham, D. T. (2004). *Ask the Cognitive Scientist: The privileged status of story.* American Educator. https://www.aft.org/periodical/american-educator/summer-2004/ask-cognitive-scientist **[secondary]**

### 1.5 Context first, cues, and reinstatement

- **Context must come before the material to help.** In the "washing clothes" studies, giving the topic or context *before* an ambiguous passage greatly improved comprehension and recall. Giving it *after* did not. Bransford, J. D., & Johnson, M. K. (1972). *Contextual prerequisites for understanding: Some investigations of comprehension and recall.* Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 11, 717–726. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(72)80006-9
- **Advance organizers help, modestly.** Ausubel, D. P. (1960). *The use of advance organizers in the learning and retention of meaningful verbal material.* Journal of Educational Psychology, 51(5), 267–272. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0046669 . A meta-analysis of 135 studies found a facilitative effect on learning and retention. Luiten, J., Ames, W., & Ackerson, G. (1980). *A meta-analysis of the effects of advance organizers on learning and retention.* American Educational Research Journal, 17(2), 211–218. https://doi.org/10.3102/00028312017002211 . For a review of overviews, pretests and organizers, see Hartley, J., & Davies, I. K. (1976). *Preinstructional strategies.* Review of Educational Research, 46(2), 239–265. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543046002239
- **Encoding specificity: a retrieval cue works if it overlaps with what was encoded.** Tulving, E., & Thomson, D. M. (1973). *Encoding specificity and retrieval processes in episodic memory.* Psychological Review, 80(5), 352–373. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0020071 . *Implication:* the book's own names, invented terms and distinctive phrases are better cues than paraphrases. The prompt already requires keeping names and terms as the book has them.
- **Reinstating the original context, even mentally, improves recall.** Smith, S. M., & Vela, E. (2001). *Environmental context-dependent memory: A review and meta-analysis.* Psychonomic Bulletin & Review, 8(2), 203–220. https://doi.org/10.3758/BF03196157

### 1.6 Summaries, testing and re-reading

- **Main-point summaries can match or beat the full text for remembering main points**, at delays from 20 minutes to 6–12 months. Reder, L. M., & Anderson, J. R. (1980). *A comparison of texts and their summaries: Memorial consequences.* Journal of Verbal Learning and Verbal Behavior, 19, 121–134. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0022-5371(80)90122-X (textbook chapters, not fiction; the authors' 1982 follow-up notes that spacing and embellishment were confounded in this study).
- **Retrieval practice beats restudy for long-term retention.** Roediger, H. L., & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). *Test-enhanced learning.* Psychological Science, 17(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x ; Rowland, C. A. (2014). *The effect of testing versus restudy on retention: A meta-analytic review of the testing effect.* Psychological Bulletin, 140(6), 1432–1463. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037559 . *Relevance is limited:* the Reader's goal is to resume reading now, not to retain the Catch-up long term. Testing someone on material they have largely forgotten mostly produces failure unless feedback follows.

### 1.7 Spoilers

- The original finding was that spoilers did not reduce, and sometimes increased, enjoyment of short stories. Leavitt, J. D., & Christenfeld, N. J. S. (2011). *Story spoilers don't spoil stories.* Psychological Science, 22(9), 1152–1154. https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797611417007
- **Later studies did not replicate it.** In a within-subjects study (N = 412), unspoiled stories were "more fun and suspenseful" and "more moving and enjoyable in general." Johnson, B. K., & Rosenbaum, J. E. (2015). *Spoiler alert: Consequences of narrative spoilers for dimensions of enjoyment, appreciation, and transportation.* Communication Research, 42(8), 1068–1088. https://doi.org/10.1177/0093650214564051 . Spoilers reduced enjoyment in a replication with different stories. Levine, W. H., Betzner, M., & Autry, K. S. (2016). *The effect of spoilers on the enjoyment of short stories.* Discourse Processes, 53(7), 513–531. https://doi.org/10.1080/0163853X.2016.1141350 (finding taken from the authors' own summary)
- *Implication:* the evidence does not license relaxing the Spoiler rules. It also supports the GLOSSARY's broad definition of Spoiler, which covers *emphasis*, not just events (see §1.9).

### 1.8 Interruption and resumption

- **Memory for goals.** A suspended goal decays and is retrieved at resumption only if cues prime it. Rehearsal before the break and cues at the point of return speed resumption. Altmann, E. M., & Trafton, J. G. (2002). *Memory for goals: An activation-based model.* Cognitive Science, 26(1), 39–83. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog2601_2
- **Preparing to resume.** People warned of an interruption encoded where they were and what they would do next, and resumed faster. Trafton, J. G., Altmann, E. M., Brock, D. P., & Mintz, F. E. (2003). *Preparing to resume an interrupted task: Effects of prospective goal encoding and retrospective rehearsal.* International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 58(5), 583–603. https://doi.org/10.1016/S1071-5819(03)00023-5 **[finding from a partial abstract and citing papers]**
- **Cues from the interrupted task reduce resumption lag.** Altmann, E. M., & Trafton, J. G. (2004). *Task interruption: Resumption lag and the role of cues.* Proceedings of the 26th Annual Conference of the Cognitive Science Society. https://escholarship.org/uc/item/18b4r661 ; Hodgetts, H. M., & Jones, D. M. (2006). *Contextual cues aid recovery from interruption: The role of associative activation.* JEP: LMC, 32(5), 1120–1132. https://doi.org/10.1037/0278-7393.32.5.1120
- **Interruptions hurt reading comprehension specifically**, and a short pre-interruption "time-out" removed the harm. Foroughi, C. K., Werner, N. E., Barragán, D., & Boehm-Davis, D. A. (2015). *Interruptions disrupt reading comprehension.* Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 144(3), 704–709. https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000074 . A citing paper (Chevet et al., 2022) reports that explicitly stated information survives the interruption but *inferred* information does not. **[secondary]** That is exactly the causal and goal inference layer the Catch-up has to rebuild.
- *Caveat:* these are lab tasks with interruptions of seconds to minutes. A paused book involves days to months. I apply the mechanism (pending goals need cues; the point of re-entry needs a cue), not the timing.

### 1.9 Practice: Kindle, X-Ray, TV recaps

- **Kindle Recaps and Story So Far (first-party).** Amazon describes Recaps as "quick refreshers" for books in a series, "like the 'Previously on…' segment" of a TV show. It later added **Story So Far**: "spoiler-free refreshers up to where you've read," covering "storylines and character arcs," for "thousands of popular fiction titles." Amazon (2025, updated 2026-07-02). *Kindle Recaps.* https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/books-and-authors/kindle-recaps-feature-ebook-series-refreshers . Amazon does not publish the anatomy or length. Press reports that Recaps are made with "GenAI and Amazon moderators" (Engadget, 2025: https://www.engadget.com/ai/amazon-will-use-ai-to-generate-recaps-for-book-series-on-the-kindle-170018503.html) **[secondary]**. *Takeaway:* the leading commercial product treats *storylines* and *character arcs* as the two core units. That matches the causal/goal and protagonist findings above.
- **Kindle X-Ray (first-party).** Since 2011, X-Ray has let readers explore the "bones of the book": all passages that mention characters, places, terms and topics, as an on-demand lookup. Amazon press release (2011-09-28). https://press.aboutamazon.com/2011/9/introducing-the-all-new-kindle-family-four-new-kindles-four-amazing-price-points . *Takeaway:* characters and terms work as **reference you look up**, separate from the narrative you read.
- **TV "previously on" recaps.** I found **no controlled experiments** on recap design or their effect on comprehension. The scholarly treatments are media-studies analyses. Mittell, J. (2015). *Complex TV: The Poetics of Contemporary Television Storytelling*, ch. "Comprehension." NYU Press. https://scalar.usc.edu/works/complex-television/comprehension (discusses how programs "trigger memories and exploit viewers' fading memories"). Bisoni, C. (2016). *The TV Recap: Knowledge, Memory, and Complex Narrative Orientation* (abstract via https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/561724) **[secondary, I could not read the full text]**. Writers' lore, which I could find only in secondary sources, says recaps select clips that set up *this episode*. **For a Catch-up, that selection rule is itself a Spoiler** (GLOSSARY: "emphasis on what later proves important"; prompt rule 3). The Catch-up must select by weight *in the Read Portion*, never by relevance to what comes next. This is the main way the TV model must not be copied.
- **Serialized fiction "story so far" conventions** (magazine serials and webfiction synopses before each instalment). I found no primary research. The convention is a short prose synopsis that ends at the cliffhanger. **[no source located; convention only]**

## 2. Recommendations

The Catch-up does two jobs that the evidence treats differently:

1. **Rebuild the situation model**: who, where, when, what caused what, who wants what. This is best done with *connected causal narrative*, preceded by a short frame (§1.1, §1.2, §1.4, §1.5).
2. **Supply cues for re-entry**: the exact moment the Reader stopped, plus the pending questions and goals. This is best placed *immediately before* the Reader returns to the book (§1.5, §1.8).

Reference material (cast, places, terms) is a third, *look-up* job (§1.9 X-Ray). It should not sit in the reading path.

### R1. Order: frame, then story, then threads, then the last scene, then "continue". Reference goes last, after a divider.

Recommended reading path:

1. **Header** (as now).
2. **Where you are**: 2–4 sentences. Cover the five event-indexing dimensions at the Resume Point: *when, where, who is present, what just happened and why, what the main characters want*. This is the advance organizer (Bransford & Johnson 1972; Ausubel 1960; Zwaan et al. 1995).
3. **The story so far**: causal narrative prose (R3).
4. **Open threads**: unanswered questions *and* characters' pending goals (R5).
5. **Right before you continue**: a close account of the last scene, ending exactly at the Resume Point, followed by the single line "Continue with Chapter N+1." This should be the last thing read, because it is the re-entry cue (Altmann & Trafton 2002, 2004; Trafton et al. 2003; Tulving & Thomson 1973).
6. `---` then **Reference**: *Characters so far* and *World and terms*, explicitly labelled as look-up material.

Why not last scene first (variant B)? Bransford & Johnson show that context must precede material for it to help. A vivid last scene read cold is harder to interpret, and Albrecht & O'Brien show the next scene is read against global context. B's useful insight, that the Reader needs the stopping moment above all, is kept in R1 by naming that moment in the 2–4 sentence frame and by making the full last scene the final thing before resuming.

**Confidence: medium.** The frame-first and cue-at-re-entry principles are well established. Applying them to a weeks-long pause is an inference, and no study compares these orderings for novels.

### R2. Give the last scene a dedicated, close account, and quote sparingly.

Write it at higher resolution than anything else: who is present, where, what was just said or done, and the emotional state at the cut. Include one or two short **verbatim** phrases from the final page when they are distinctive. They act as retrieval cues matched to how the Reader encoded the scene (Tulving & Thomson 1973; Smith & Vela 2001). Keep quotes few: surface wording is the weakest memory trace (Kintsch et al. 1990), so quotes work as cues, not as content.

**Confidence: medium** for a dedicated close last-scene section (resumption-cue literature, plus Kindle's "up to where you've read" framing). **Low** for verbatim quotes specifically (an inference from encoding specificity; untested for this use).

### R3. Write the plot as causal narrative prose, organized by story arc, not by chapter.

- Use connected prose with explicit causal and goal links ("because", "so", "in order to", "which led to"). Prioritize events on the causal chain and drop dead ends (Trabasso & van den Broek 1985; Trabasso & Sperry 1985; Black & Bower 1980; Mar et al. 2021).
- Group by arc or part. A per-chapter list breaks the causal chain into fragments and puts equal weight on unequal events (Kintsch & van Dijk 1978 levels effect). If chapter anchors are wanted, add them inline, e.g. "(Ch. 4)", rather than making chapters the structure.
- Bold each character's name at first mention, so the prose doubles as a light index.

**Confidence: medium-high** that causal, goal-centred content is what to prioritize (a strong, consistent literature). **Medium** that prose beats bullet lists for this content: the narrative-advantage meta-analysis compares genres, not formats of the same content.

### R4. Present characters by goal, relationship and what the Reader knows. Order them by weight in the Read Portion. Keep them in a compact reference list.

Each entry: name, role, **what they want or are doing at the Resume Point**, key relationships, and what the Reader knows or suspects about them, phrased exactly as unresolved as the book leaves it. This follows the protagonist and intentionality dimensions of situation models (Zwaan et al. 1995; Rinck & Bower 1995) and Kindle's "character arcs" unit. Put the list in Reference (R1) because it is look-up material, as in X-Ray. Use a list, not a wide table (tables render poorly in many chat UIs and on phones; practical judgment, no source). Order by prominence *in the Read Portion*, never by eventual importance (Spoiler rule 3).

**Confidence: medium** (content of an entry: well grounded; placement and format: practice-based).

### R5. Make Open threads include pending goals, phrased neutrally.

The memory-for-goals model says suspended goals decay and must be cued to be resumed (Altmann & Trafton 2002). In a story, the Reader's pending "goals" are its open questions and characters' unfinished intentions. Interrupted readers lose *inferred* links more than explicit facts (Foroughi et al. 2015; Chevet et al. 2022 [secondary]). Keep questions and intentions exactly as unresolved as the text leaves them. Place the section right before the last scene so the questions are active as the Reader re-enters.

**Confidence: medium-low** (sound mechanism, but a big jump from lab tasks to novels).

### R6. Length: compress hard, scale by book length and detail level, and spend words on the frame and the last scene.

No study sets an optimal recap length for fiction. What the evidence supports: main-point summaries can serve memory as well as or better than full text (Reder & Anderson 1980), and gist-level information is what readers keep (Kintsch & van Dijk 1978). Suggested budgets (judgment, not evidence): frame 40–80 words; story so far 250–1,200 words depending on book and detail level; open threads 3–7 items; last scene 120–300 words; reference as long as the cast needs. "Quick" should shrink the story so far, never the frame or the last scene.

**Confidence: low** for the specific numbers. **Medium** for the principle that the frame and the last scene are the protected minimum.

### R7. Layering: keep a short top layer, but avoid three full repeated layers.

The 2–4 sentence "Where you are" frame *is* the 30-second layer: a macrostructure-level summary and advance organizer (Kintsch & van Dijk 1978; Luiten et al. 1980). A full 30 s / 3 min / full stack (variant E) lets readers stop early. But it repeats the same events three times, pushes the last scene to the very bottom of a long document, and has no direct evidence behind it. The existing Quick / Standard / Detailed setup choice already gives readers control over depth without the repetition.

**Confidence: low-medium.**

### R8. Do not build retrieval quizzes into the document. Offer one afterwards at most.

The testing effect is real (Roediger & Karpicke 2006; Rowland 2014), but it targets long-term retention of studied material. The Reader needs immediate resumption, and testing largely forgotten material without restudy produces failure. An optional "want a quick quiz on what you've read?" in the follow-up phase is defensible. Inside the Catch-up it is not.

**Confidence: low** (no direct evidence either way for this use).

### R9. Never select or emphasize by relevance to what comes next.

TV recaps select for the upcoming episode. A Catch-up must select by causal weight *within* the Read Portion. Spoiler research after 2011 found that spoilers reduce enjoyment and suspense (Johnson & Rosenbaum 2015; Levine et al. 2016), which supports the current strict rules.

**Confidence: high** for keeping the rules. The underlying point is a design constraint more than an empirical claim.

## 3. How variants A–E score

Scale: ++ strongly supported, + supported, 0 neutral or unknown, − counter to evidence.

| Criterion (evidence) | A Current | B Resume-first | C "Previously on" prose | D Timeline tables | E Layered |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Frame before detail (Bransford & Johnson; Ausubel) | + "Where you are" first | − last scene before context | + (if it opens with a frame) | 0 | ++ 30 s layer |
| Causal/goal narrative for plot (Trabasso; Black & Bower; Mar) | − per-chapter bullets | + short prose "How you got here" | ++ | − cells fragment causality | 0 / + depends on layer |
| Last scene as the re-entry cue, last before resuming (Altmann & Trafton; Tulving) | ++ ends with it | − first, far from re-entry | + ends at the moment of stopping | + has it | − buried at the end of "Full detail" |
| Open threads near re-entry (memory for goals) | 0 middle | + early | + after the prose | + table with source chapter | 0 |
| Characters as goal-centred look-up (event indexing; X-Ray) | 0 inline list in the reading path | + reference appendix | + compact cast after | + cast table (lookup, but appearance-by-chapter adds little) | + |
| Gist over detail / compression (Kintsch & van Dijk; Reder & Anderson) | 0 | + | + | − encourages exhaustive cells | 0 three times the content |
| Chat/phone rendering (practical) | + | + | ++ | − wide tables | + |
| Spoiler risk from structure | low | low | medium: prose invites ironic framing and emphasis, needs rule 2/3 discipline | medium: "chapter that raised it" columns can tempt titles and weighting | low |

**Overall:** none of the five is the evidence-best as-is. The best-supported anatomy is a **hybrid**:

- E's short top frame,
- C's causal narrative prose for the story so far,
- A's dedicated last-scene section, placed last in the reading path,
- B's separation of reference material into an appendix,
- D's tables dropped from the reading path. A chapter anchor such as "(Ch. 4)" inline is enough for locating.

A and C come closest. A needs its plot rewritten from per-chapter bullets into arc-level causal prose, and its Characters and World sections moved below the last scene as reference. C needs an explicit, separately headed last-scene section and a 2–4 sentence frame on top.

### Where the evidence is thin

- **No direct study of resuming a novel after a pause, or of recap anatomy.** Every recommendation is transferred from comprehension, memory or interruption research.
- **Prose vs list for identical content is untested here.** The narrative advantage (Mar et al. 2021) is a genre comparison.
- **Length and layering are essentially judgment calls.**
- **TV recap and serial-fiction practice has no experimental literature** I could find. Media-studies sources are interpretive.
- **Interruption research uses seconds-to-minutes gaps** in lab tasks.
- A cheap way to close the gap: run the prototype's variants (or the hybrid) with a few Readers on a real paused book and measure (a) self-rated readiness, (b) time to "feel back in" after resuming, and (c) a few comprehension questions about the next chapter.

## Decision (2026-11-18)

After comparing the prototype variants (branch `prototype/catch-up-anatomy`), the Reader chose **variant F**: The story so far → Where you are → Open threads → "Continue with the next chapter" → divider → Characters so far, World and terms (both as `**Name**: description` lists).

It follows R2–R5 and R7–R9. It deliberately departs from R1 in two ways:
- **No separate frame before the story.** The frame and the last scene were merged into "Where you are", because they repeated each other. The story's first sentence does the orienting.
- **Open threads, not the last scene, is the last thing read** before the book.

If testing with real Readers shows that the story so far feels cold to start, revisit R1.
