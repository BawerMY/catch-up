How to bring a paused book back to mind

What research on memory and reading says about the best way to rebuild a Reader's context, and what that means for the Catch-up's anatomy. This page summarizes docs/research/catch-up-anatomy.md, which has the full citations.

Honesty first: no study tests "a document that helps someone resume a novel after a pause of weeks". Everything here is transferred from nearby research: how stories are understood, how memory works, and how people resume interrupted tasks. Each recommendation carries a confidence label.

1 · What you actually remember from a book

Reading builds three layers of memory, and they fade at very different speeds. What lasts is a mental model of the situation, not the words.

Bar lengths are illustrative, not measured. Kintsch et al. 1990; Fletcher & Chrysler 1990; Zwaan & Radvansky 1998.

Readers organize that situation model around five dimensions, and update it whenever one of them changes:

When timeWhere spaceWho protagonistWhy causalityWants goals

Event-indexing model: Zwaan, Langston & Graesser 1995.

The Catch-up's job is to rebuild this model, not to repeat the text. A reader who remembers every event but not why they happened isn't ready to continue.

2 · Six findings that shape the design

strong evidence

The cause-and-effect chain is what sticks

Events linked by cause and effect to other events are recalled more, summarized more and rated more important. Dead-end events fade.

Tell the plot as a chain of "because" and "so", and drop dead ends.

Trabasso & van den Broek 1985 · Black & Bower 1980

strong evidence

Context must come before detail

When readers got a passage's topic before reading it, comprehension and recall jumped. Getting the same topic after reading didn't help.

Open with a short frame: when, where, who, what just happened, who wants what.

Bransford & Johnson 1972 · Luiten et al. 1980

medium (indirect)

Stories beat essays for memory

A meta-analysis of more than 33,000 participants found narrative texts are understood and recalled better than expository ones. It compared genres, not prose versus bullet lists of the same content.

Write the story so far as connected prose, not a per-chapter list.

Mar et al. 2021

medium (lab tasks)

Resuming needs a cue at the point of return

Unfinished goals fade during an interruption. People pick a task back up faster when a cue from where they left off greets them. Interrupted readers lose inferred links more than stated facts.

End with the last scene, so it's the final thing read before chapter X+1.

Altmann & Trafton 2002 · Foroughi et al. 2015

medium

The book's own words are the best cues

A memory cue works when it matches how the memory was formed. The book's names, invented terms and distinctive phrases trigger recall better than paraphrases.

Keep names and terms exactly as the book has them, and quote one or two short lines from the last page.

Tulving & Thomson 1973

consistent

Spoilers do hurt

A famous 2011 study found that spoilers don't spoil stories, but later studies with more participants did not replicate it: spoiled stories were less suspenseful and less enjoyable.

Keep the strict rules. Unlike TV recaps, which pick clips that set up the next episode, never pick content by what comes next.

Johnson & Rosenbaum 2015 · Levine et al. 2016

Practice agrees: Kindle's Story So Far describes itself as "spoiler-free refreshers up to where you've read," built around storylines and character arcs. Kindle X-Ray keeps characters and terms as separate look-up material, outside the reading flow. (Amazon)

3 · The anatomy the evidence points to

The research separates two jobs: a reading path, read once top to bottom to rebuild the model and end at the point of return, and a reference appendix to look things up. Examples use Jekyll and Hyde through chapter 7.

Header as now

Book, Edition, where the text came from, the stopping point, and "no spoilers past here".

↓

1. Where you are 40–80 words · never shortened

The frame: when, where, who, what just happened and why, who wants what.

Months after Carew's murder, Hyde has vanished and Lanyon is dead after a shock he wouldn't explain. Jekyll has shut himself away again, and Utterson, still determined to understand Jekyll's tie to Hyde, has just watched him recoil in terror at his window.
↓

2. The story so far 250–1,200 words · grows or shrinks with detail level

Cause-and-effect prose grouped by story arc, not chapter. Names in bold at first mention, with chapter anchors inline.

The will. Because Enfield's story names a man called Hyde, Utterson grows alarmed: Jekyll's will leaves everything to Hyde (Ch. 1–2). So he seeks Hyde out…
↓

3. Open threads 3–7 items

Unanswered questions and characters' unfinished intentions, phrased exactly as open as the book leaves them.

What hold does Hyde have over Jekyll? · Utterson means to find out what Jekyll is hiding.
↓

4. Right before you continue 120–300 words · never shortened

The last scene at the highest detail: who, where, what was said, the mood at the cut, plus one or two short quotes. Then: "Continue with the next chapter."

…the window was thrust down. They walked on in silence until Utterson said, "God forgive us."
— reference: look up as needed —

Characters so far

Each entry gives the character's role, what they want now, their relationships, and what the Reader knows or suspects. Ordered by weight in the text read so far, never by later importance. A list, not a wide table.

World and terms

Places, factions and invented words. Omitted if there are none.

What changes from today

✚ Plot becomes cause-and-effect prose grouped by arc (it was per-chapter bullets)

✚ Open threads include characters' intentions

✚ Characters and World move below the last scene, as reference

✚ Short quotes in the last scene

✚ Quick mode shortens only the story so far

Deliberately not done

✗ Last scene first: context has to come before detail

✗ Three repeated layers: too much repetition, no evidence

✗ Quizzes: they help long-term learning, not resuming now

✗ Wide tables: break up cause and effect, and render poorly on phones and in chat

No variant wins as-is. The best-supported anatomy is a hybrid:

E's short top frame C's cause-and-effect prose A's last scene, placed last B's reference appendix D's tables dropped

C's spoiler risk is "medium" because flowing prose invites ironic framing ("little did he know"), so spoiler rules 2 and 3 need extra care. That's one more reason the prompt's check before sending matters.

5 · Where the evidence is thin

  • No direct study of resuming a novel or of recap anatomy. Everything is transferred from nearby research.
  • Prose versus list for the same content is untested. The narrative advantage compares genres.
  • Word budgets and layering are judgment calls.
  • Interruption studies use pauses of seconds to minutes, not weeks.
  • TV recaps have no experimental literature, only media-studies analysis.

A cheap way to close the gap: give a few Readers with a real paused book either the hybrid or variant A, then measure (a) how ready they feel, (b) how long it takes them to feel back into the book, and (c) a few comprehension questions on the next chapter.
